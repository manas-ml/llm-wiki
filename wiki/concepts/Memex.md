# Memex

**Domain**: information science, history of computing
**Also called**: Memory Extender

## Definition

The Memex (Memory Extender) was a hypothetical personal knowledge device described by [[Vannevar Bush]] in his 1945 essay "As We May Think." It would store a person's books, records, and communications and allow them to create associative trails — links between related items — that could be navigated and shared. The Memex is widely considered the conceptual precursor to hypertext, the World Wide Web, and personal knowledge management systems.

## How It Works

Bush envisioned the Memex as a desk with microfilm storage. Key properties:
- **Personal and curated** — each person's Memex reflected their own reading and thinking, not a shared index
- **Associative trails** — the user could link any item to any other, creating paths through the knowledge base that mirrored associative human thought
- **Trail sharing** — trails could be extracted and shared with others, passing on not just documents but the connections between them
- **Active curation** — the value was in the trails, not the raw documents

The part Bush couldn't solve: **who maintains it?** Building and keeping associative trails current is enormous manual labor. His vision assumed a dedicated human effort that proved impractical.

## Connection to the LLM Wiki Pattern

The [[LLM Wiki Pattern]] is spiritually closest to Bush's Memex among modern implementations:
- Private and actively curated (not a public search index)
- The connections between documents (wikilinks, cross-references) are as valuable as the documents themselves
- The LLM solves Bush's unsolved problem — it does the maintenance that no human wants to do

The web, by contrast, diverged from Bush's vision: it became public, link-following rather than trail-building, and the curation burden fell on search engines rather than individuals.
